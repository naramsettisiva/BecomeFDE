How the day is built

Every one of the 24 days has the same shape: warm-up, learn, build, teach-back, ship. The learn block is a written module you read before touching the keyboard; the proportions shift as the course moves from building to teaching, and the coloured bar on each card shows you how at a glance.

Learn first, then build

Each day opens with a teaching module — learn/DAY_NN_LEARN.md — that explains the mechanism, works examples on paper, names the misconceptions, and ends in a self-check. Then you build. The order matters twice: you learn faster, and you can only teach the part you were taught. Someone who learned purely by doing can demonstrate; someone who understands the mechanism can answer the question nobody prepared them for.

Build it by hand before you import it

You write cosine similarity before you touch a vector database. You write the agent loop before you touch LangGraph. An FDE debugs other people's stacks under time pressure, and a trainer who only knows the wrapper cannot answer the good questions.

Every teach-back is recorded

Five to twenty minutes, screen recording, every day. You cannot become a trainer by reading. You become one by hearing yourself be unclear and fixing it. Twenty-one recordings is the evidence that you improved.

Every confusion gets logged

Your learning log is not a diary — it is curriculum source material. Every line you write while confused is a place a future student will get stuck. Day 22 mines the whole file into a lesson plan.

Local models by default, paid where it earns it

Ollama runs the bulk of the course on your MacBook for nothing. Hosted models appear only where the lab is marked paid — usually judging, comparison, or a production path. Total spend lands around $40–85.

The AWS lane

A second lane runs beside the core one: every capability you build from scratch, you also rebuild on managed AWS services. Roughly 45 minutes a day, traded from the core lane's stretch goals rather than added on top. Hard budget: $50.

Why two lanes and not one

Build-from-scratch is what lets you teach. Managed services are what lets you deploy at a client. Doing both is the only way to earn the sentence this lane exists for: "Bedrock Knowledge Bases gets you 80% of this in an afternoon — here's the 20% it doesn't do, what it costs at your volume, and the failure mode you'll hit."

The budget is the curriculum

$50 across 24 days is tight, and that's the point — cost discipline is the FDE skill clients notice first. A forgotten resource in a client account is a career event, not an inconvenience. Every lab writes its teardown before it writes its setup.

AWS has no hard spending cap

Budgets and Cost Anomaly Detection read Cost Explorer, which lags up to 24 hours. A forgotten ml.g5.xlarge bills ~$34 before an alert fires. Budget Actions deny new spend; they don't stop existing spend. make aws-nuke is the only zero-latency control.

macOS throughout

Homebrew, zsh, BSD date and sed, bash 3.2-safe scripts, and Apple Silicon notes wherever architecture matters — Lambda packaging, Docker builds for Fargate and AgentCore. The gotcha table is in AWS_SETUP.md.

The asymmetry that shapes every architecture decision

Left column: what a forgotten resource costs. Right column: what this course actually uses. That gap is the whole lesson — and the right architecture for a $50 budget is very often the right architecture for a client's pilot, because a pilot at 7,000 queries a month has the same shape as your bootcamp.

Where this goes further, and where it doesn't

Beyond the standard syllabus

  • Discovery and scoping — the six-area framework, the scoping canvas, and the four hard client conversations (Day 19)
  • Curriculum design — failure-organised lesson architecture, mined from your own learning log (Day 22)
  • Live delivery — a full 60-minute lesson, one take, plus a hostile Q&A drill and a facilitation playbook (Day 23)
  • Handover discipline — runbook, capacity doc, security answers, and an enterprise VPC design (Day 17)
  • Cost engineering — a model built from four weeks of your own measured token data, not estimates (Day 18)

Named gaps — not skipped silently

  • Fine-tuning and adaptation — LoRA, preference tuning, when it beats prompting
  • Self-hosted GPU serving — vLLM, quantisation, throughput and capacity planning
  • Multimodal — vision on scanned freight paperwork, voice interfaces

Day 24 asks you to pick two of these to close in the following 90 days. Not three. A plan that closes every gap closes none.
